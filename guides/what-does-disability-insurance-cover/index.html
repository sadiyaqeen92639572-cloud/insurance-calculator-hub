What Does Disability Insurance Actually Cover?

The fine print that determines whether a policy actually pays out when you need it.

Written & fact-checked by the CoverFormula editorial team · Last reviewed 2026-07-29

The Basic Promise

Disability insurance replaces a portion of your income — typically 60-70% — if illness or injury prevents you from working. That sounds simple, but the details of "prevents you from working" and "how much you get, after tax" vary enormously between policies, and those details matter more than the headline benefit amount.

Own-Occupation vs. Any-Occupation

This is the single most important distinction in disability insurance. An own-occupation policy pays out if you can't perform the specific duties of your own job — a surgeon who loses fine motor control in their hands but could still do desk work would still qualify. An any-occupation policy only pays if you can't work in any job reasonably suited to your education and experience — a much higher bar. Own-occupation policies cost more but are dramatically more valuable for specialized professions.

Taxable vs. Tax-Free Benefits

If you pay your own premiums with after-tax dollars, your benefit is received tax-free. If your employer pays the premium (common with group disability plans), the benefit is typically taxable income — meaning a "60% of income" group policy might only replace 40-45% of your take-home pay after tax. This is why many people supplement an employer group policy with an individual policy.

The Elimination Period

The elimination period is the waiting time — commonly 30, 60, or 90 days — between the start of a disability and when benefit payments begin. A longer elimination period lowers your premium but means you need more emergency savings to bridge the gap before benefits kick in.

What Most Policies Exclude

  • Pre-existing conditions, usually for a lookback period defined in the policy.
  • Disabilities resulting from illegal activity or self-inflicted injury.
  • Mental health and substance-related claims are frequently capped at a shorter benefit period (often 24 months) rather than paying to the full benefit term.

Does Your Group Policy Cover Enough?

Often not fully — many group policies replace only 50-60% of base salary, may exclude bonuses or commissions, and are usually taxable if your employer paid the premiums.
